How you really learn to understand a language

Understanding a language rests on three things: knowing the words, seeing how sentences are built, and working out what you don't know yet. Masimera trains all three, every day, in 15 minutes.

Hear, not just read

Knowing a word is not the same as catching it in a spoken sentence. Text and audio together train both.

15 minutes a day

Meeting words again and again, and recalling them, makes them stick far better than cramming.

Where do you stand?

With 3,000 word families, you understand about 96% of everyday conversation
Share of the words of native conversation you know, by vocabulary size

The first few thousand words do most of the work: with 2,000 word families you already know 9 words in 10 of what native speakers say. Beyond 3,000, each new word adds little to everyday understanding.

But knowing a word is not the same as hearing it. In real speech, words run into each other: there are no gaps between them, and the sentence goes by only once. These percentages assume you can tell where each word starts and ends, and follow how the sentence is built. For most learners, that is the real hurdle. That's why every Masimera lesson pairs the text with its audio: first you see the words, then you learn to hear them.

The levels are close together in words: each step is a few hundred to a thousand new words. A short lesson every day, with 20 to 30 words, covers that ground steadily.

Three layers, trained every day

1. Knowing the words

What research says

A word sticks after meeting it several times: around 10 encounters give strong results. Recalling a word from memory fixes it far better than rereading it (61% remembered a week later, versus 40%).

What Masimera does

The most common words first, the rest translated. Every word comes back in the text, two example sentences, the audio and the quiz, which makes you recall it.

2. Seeing how sentences are built

What research says

We pick up grammar by meeting the same patterns again and again, more than by learning rules.

What Masimera does

Short sentences, one idea each, so the structure stands out. The same patterns come back every day. Each verb shows its dictionary form, and a full translation lets you check you understood the sentence, not just the words.

3. Working out the rest

What research says

You can guess an unknown word from context only if you understand almost everything around it: 95% of the words for some readers, 98% for most.

What Masimera does

Every text is tuned to stay in that zone. Translations are hidden by default: you try to guess first, then tap the word to check.

Little, but every day

Spreading learning over time makes it stick far better than cramming. That's why Masimera is one short lesson each morning, built on a real local news story: you remember better what actually interests you, and you want to come back tomorrow.
